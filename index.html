What you are seeing

Fluid sticks to the plate surface (no-slip condition), so speed is zero at the wall. A thin layer forms where speed rises from zero to the free-stream value. This layer is the boundary layer. It grows thicker along the plate. Past the critical Reynolds number it becomes turbulent and grows much faster.

A · The experiment

Fluid flows over a thin, smooth flat plate at speed U∞. At the plate surface the fluid speed is zero because of the no-slip condition. Above the surface, the speed rises smoothly until it reaches U∞.

The thin region where this change happens is the boundary layer. It starts at zero thickness at the leading edge and grows thicker along the plate, because viscosity slows more and more fluid.

B · Scientific principles

No-slip condition
Fluid in contact with a stationary wall has zero velocity relative to it: u = v = 0 at y = 0.
Conservation of mass
For incompressible 2D flow: ∂u/∂x + ∂v/∂y = 0
Conservation of momentum
The Navier–Stokes equations: ρ(∂u/∂t + u·∇u) = −∇p + μ∇²u. Inside a thin layer with zero pressure gradient, Prandtl's simplification gives u ∂u/∂x + v ∂u/∂y = ν ∂²u/∂y²
Reynolds number
The ratio of inertial to viscous effects. It tells you how thick the layer is relative to x and whether the flow is laminar or turbulent.

C · Scientists

Ludwig Prandtl (1904)
Introduced boundary-layer theory. He showed that viscous effects matter only in a thin layer near the surface, so the outer flow can be treated separately as nearly inviscid.
Paul Richard Heinrich Blasius (1908)
Prandtl's student. He found the similarity solution for the laminar boundary layer on a flat plate, which this simulation solves numerically.
Theodore von Kármán
Derived the momentum integral equation (1921), the basis of approximate methods such as the cubic profile shown here. He also made major contributions to turbulent boundary-layer theory.

Blasius solution (laminar) and 1/7-power law (turbulent)

Blasius solution · laminar boundary layer

Describes the steady, two-dimensional laminar boundary layer on a smooth flat plate with zero pressure gradient. It is a classical similarity solution of Prandtl's boundary-layer equations: the profile has the same shape at every x once y is scaled with √(νx/U∞).

η = y√U∞/νx,   u/U∞ = f′(η),   f‴ + ½ f f″ = 0

η is the similarity variable. The boundary conditions are f(0) = f′(0) = 0 (no slip at the wall) and f′ → 1 far from the wall (free stream). So the speed starts at zero at the plate and rises to 0.99 U∞ at η ≈ 4.91.

The simulation solves this equation with RK4 and uses f′(η) for every laminar velocity profile.

One-seventh-power law · turbulent boundary layer

uU∞ = (yδ)1/7   for y ≤ δ

u = local speed, U∞ = free-stream speed, y = distance from the wall, δ = turbulent boundary-layer thickness.

An empirical approximation, fitted to measured profiles over smooth surfaces. It gives a fuller profile than the laminar one: speed rises steeply near the wall and changes slowly further out. This makes it useful for simple estimates of δ and drag.

It is not an exact solution of the turbulent Navier–Stokes equations. It also gives an infinite slope at the wall, so wall shear is taken from a separate friction correlation (Cf,x = 0.059/Rex1/5 in this simulation).

E · Laminar and turbulent flow

Laminar layers flow in smooth sheets. Momentum moves across the layer only by viscosity, so the layer stays thin: δ grows with x1/2.

Turbulent layers mix strongly. Fast fluid is carried toward the wall, so the profile is fuller, the layer grows faster (about x4/5) and the wall shear stress is higher.

Transition starts near a critical Reynolds number. 5×105 is a common textbook value, but it is only an approximate criterion. Roughness, free-stream disturbances and vibration can move it lower or higher.

F · Assumptions and limitations

  • Smooth, flat plate with zero pressure gradient.
  • Steady, incompressible flow.
  • Constant fluid properties at 20 °C.
  • Transition occurs at a single point; turbulent values use empirical correlations.
  • Boundary-layer theory needs δ ≪ x, so results are not reliable at low ReL.

Real experiments can differ because of surface roughness, free-stream turbulence, temperature changes and other conditions.

G · Applications

  • Aircraft wings: skin-friction drag and stall.
  • Ship hulls: frictional resistance.
  • Wind turbine blades.
  • Heat transfer from heated or cooled surfaces.
  • Flow over industrial equipment, ducts and heat exchangers.

Key takeaways

  • The no-slip condition creates a thin layer where speed rises from 0 to U∞.
  • The Reynolds number controls how thick the layer is and whether it is laminar or turbulent.
  • A laminar layer grows with √x; a turbulent layer grows faster and causes more drag.
  • δ99, δ* and θ are three different ways to measure the layer, each with its own meaning.
  • The formulas used here are classical or empirical results for ideal conditions.
